The problem, from our own tree

The root we can actually act on is awareness.

Climate stress on Ateneo's urban forest has three roots in our problem tree: infrastructure taking soil and growing space, policy in which resilience and native biodiversity lose to other priorities, and awareness and knowledge — limited understanding of how species diversity builds resilience, and of what heat, drought and extreme weather do to a forest.

Students are not positioned to fix the first two directly. We are positioned to fix the third, and the third is upstream of the other two.

The chain we are claiming, stated so it can be argued with:

  • A community that cannot name its forest…
  • …is not a constituency for that forest…
  • …when a car park is proposed over it.

What we are not claiming

Not a planting drive Not a carbon-credit product

Not a replacement for the AIS inventory

AIS has already counted the trees. Their figures appear throughout the app cited to them. What the inventory does not have is a count and a location contributed by the people who walk past the tree — and that is the only thing this app produces.

The gamification follows Ateneo's own published work — Rodrigo, Favis & Cuyegkeng (2021), RECIPE / Meaningful Gamification — which is why there is reflection and progression here, and no leaderboard.

What it is

A rotating world of finds appears across campus every thirty minutes.
Walk to one, photograph it, and it enters your journal.

  • 1,098 species with their own 3D model, from a real iNaturalist sweep of the campus box — 627 plants, 215 insects, 122 fungi, 43 arachnids, 41 birds, 50 others.
  • 68 walkable sectors, cut as faces of the real OpenStreetMap path network and with their greenness measured off satellite imagery, not inferred from the absence of a building.
  • Works offline. Shell, character models and the world data are precached — verified with the network cut, not assumed.
  • No account, no app store. A QR at the booth opens it.

The two rules we designed against

Rarity is data. Nothing ranks anyone.

Rarity is data

How rare a species is comes from its own iNaturalist observation count inside the campus box. Seen 96 times → Common. Seen once → “Once on campus”, and the card says where the number came from.

How often each band appears — 55 / 25 / 15 / 5 — is the one number we invented, and the card says that too.

We could have made rarity up. Deriving it from real observation gaps is what makes a walk produce data instead of points.

Nothing ranks anyone

Thirteen badges, every one earned by doing the thing the app is for. No currency, nothing purchasable, no loot-box odds, no expiry.

No leaderboard — and no field in the data model one could be built from. The multiplayer sync carries a name, a stage and a count; there is nothing to sort by. A test in the build refuses any badge whose name or description promises a fact outside your own journal.

Personal progression No public rank

What it produces, and what it does not

The honest half of the pitch.

What it produces

Every logged find carries species, count, coordinate, accuracy and time, exportable as CSV and GeoJSON. That pair — a count and a location — is exactly what the existing inventory does not have, and it is the input to an annual monitoring report.

Photos and notes never leave the device. Only species, count and location are ever shared, and only if a server is running.

Built to be checked

208 automated tests, typecheck and lint green. Several exist specifically to stop the app claiming more than it measured.

What we do not have yet

  • The AIS species-per-sector inventory. 6 of 68 walkable sectors name anything to find today.
  • The ADMUNAV path graph. Not shared; we cut our own from OSM instead.
  • Origin data. 9 of 1,098 species carry a native/exotic label, so the app's intended bias toward native species is inert today.
  • A test on a real handset.
  • Any usage number. We measure none of our own objectives yet and will not quote one we did not count.

The ask

Three things, and none of them is money.

  • The AIS inventory — or a decision that it will not be shared, so we can plan around its absence instead of waiting on it.
  • One faculty or office sponsor willing to own an annual biodiversity monitoring report built on student-collected observations.
  • Mentor feedback on metric honesty — which of our four objectives should we commit to measuring, given we measure none of them today?

Objectives this serves, from the deck as submitted: consult ≥20 students and stakeholders by Q3 AY 2026–27; map ≥80% of identified trees in a selected campus area by Q3; develop and pilot the website by Q4 — already done, two quarters early; engage ≥100 students by end of AY.
